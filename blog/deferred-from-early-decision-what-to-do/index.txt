<p>Being deferred is disappointing, but it isn't a no. A deferral means the college wants to see your application again alongside the regular pool. Thousands of students are admitted after a deferral every year. What you do in the next few weeks can matter.</p>

<h2>First: read the letter carefully</h2>
<p>Colleges handle deferrals differently. Some invite updates; some ask you not to send anything beyond mid-year grades. Follow the instructions exactly. Ignoring a "please don't send additional materials" request doesn't help.</p>

<h2>If you were deferred from Early Decision</h2>
<p>A deferral releases you from the binding commitment. You're free to apply Early Decision II elsewhere, and that's worth considering seriously if you have another school you'd happily commit to. See our guide to <a href="/blog/early-decision-vs-early-action/">early application rounds</a>.</p>

<h2>Write a letter of continued interest (if allowed)</h2>
<p>A letter of continued interest (LOCI) is a short, specific note to the admissions office. A strong one:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Confirms the school is still a top choice</strong> (and says you'll attend if admitted, only if that's true).</li>
<li><strong>Shares meaningful updates</strong> since you applied: new results, leadership, research, a strong semester.</li>
<li><strong>Adds one or two specific reasons</strong> you fit that weren't in your original application.</li>
<li><strong>Stays under a page.</strong> Admissions officers read a lot of these.</li>
</ol>
<p>Send it to your regional admissions officer if you know who that is, or through the applicant portal.</p>
