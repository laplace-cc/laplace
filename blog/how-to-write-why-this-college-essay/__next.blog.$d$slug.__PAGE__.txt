<p>Nearly every selective college asks some version of "Why do you want to attend our school?" It's one of the most common supplements, and one of the easiest to get wrong. The test is simple: if you could swap in another college's name and the essay still works, it isn't specific enough.</p>

<h2>What admissions officers are looking for</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Fit:</strong> will you use what's actually here?</li>
<li><strong>Research:</strong> do you know the school beyond its rankings and reputation?</li>
<li><strong>Contribution:</strong> what will you add to the community?</li>
</ul>

<h2>A three-part framework</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Start with you.</strong> Open with a specific interest, question, or experience from your own life. This anchors the essay in who you are.</li>
<li><strong>Connect to specific resources.</strong> Name two or three concrete things at the school that serve that interest: a course, a lab or research group, a program, a curriculum feature, a student organization. Explain how you'd use each one.</li>
<li><strong>Show what you'd contribute.</strong> End with what you'll bring, such as a club you'd join or start, a perspective you'd add, or work you'd continue.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Where to find specifics</h2>
<ul>
<li>Department pages and course catalogs.</li>
<li>Faculty research pages in your area of interest.</li>
<li>Student newspaper and club pages, which show what campus life really looks like.</li>
<li>Virtual sessions and conversations with current students.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Praising rankings, location, or "prestige."</strong> The school knows it's well regarded.</li>
<li><strong>Listing resources without connecting them to you.</strong> Name-dropping a professor isn't fit.</li>
<li><strong>Repeating the brochure.</strong> If the sentence could appear on the school's homepage, cut it.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring the word count.</strong> Shorter prompts need even more specificity per sentence.</li>
</ul>
