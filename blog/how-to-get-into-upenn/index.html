How to Get Into UPenn: What Wharton and Penn Arts & Sciences Actually Want

Penn admitted roughly 7% of applicants in its most recent cycle. That number sounds manageable compared to Harvard or Columbia — but Penn's admissions process is more complicated than most, because you are not applying to "Penn." You are applying to a specific school within Penn, and that distinction shapes everything from how your application is read to what supplemental essays you are asked to write.

Understanding the differences between Wharton, the College of Arts & Sciences, the School of Engineering and Applied Science, and the dual degree programs is not optional for a competitive Penn application. Here is what we have learned working with students who got in and students who didn't.

Wharton vs. Penn Arts & Sciences: These Are Different Applications

The most important thing to understand about Penn is that each undergraduate school has its own culture, its own supplemental essay prompts, and its own admissions priorities. Wharton is looking for students who can demonstrate a genuine, grounded interest in business — not just "I want to be successful" but specific evidence of commercial curiosity: internships, entrepreneurial projects, investing clubs, consulting competitions, or original research into a business question.

Penn Arts & Sciences (CAS) is looking for intellectual range and academic seriousness. The college has strong programs in economics, philosophy, international relations, and the sciences, and it values students who have pursued ideas across disciplines. The supplemental essays for CAS ask about your intellectual interests differently than Wharton's prompts, and the committee is reading for different things.

The most common mistake we see is students who are genuinely interested in business applying to CAS because they think Wharton is "too hard to get into." Penn readers have seen this pattern thousands of times, and a business-focused profile paired with a CAS application creates cognitive dissonance that hurts rather than helps. Apply where your interests genuinely lie, and write your supplemental essays accordingly.

The M&T Program: Penn's Most Selective Offering

The Jerome Fisher Program in Management & Technology (M&T) is a coordinated dual degree that grants students both a Wharton degree and a degree from the School of Engineering and Applied Science in four years. It is one of the most selective programs at any university in the country — acceptance rates are estimated to be well under 5% of an already-competitive Penn applicant pool.

M&T admits students who have demonstrated genuine excellence and interest in both business and technology, not students who are strong in one area and adequate in the other. The ideal M&T applicant has done something that genuinely required both technical and commercial thinking — founded a tech startup, built a product for a real market, conducted research with business applications, or led a technical team through a problem with organizational complexity.

If you are applying to M&T, your application needs to answer a specific question: why do you need both simultaneously? The joint degree structure at Penn is unusual — most schools offer business and engineering separately, with limited coordination. Your M&T essays should make clear why the integration is important to what you want to do, not just that you are interested in both fields.

The "Why Penn" Essay Is the Most Important Thing You Will Write

Penn requires a supplemental essay asking why you want to attend Penn specifically. This essay is the single most important piece of supplemental writing in your application, and the bar for specificity is high. Penn readers have read thousands of essays that mention Wharton's reputation, Penn's location in Philadelphia, or the value of a "Penn network." None of those things distinguish you.

What distinguishes a strong "Why Penn" essay is specificity that could only have come from genuine research. Reference a professor whose work you have actually read. Describe a specific program — the Wharton Social Impact Initiative, the Huntsman Program in International Studies and Business, the Vagelos Program in Life Sciences & Management, the Pennovation Center — and articulate why that specific resource matters to your specific goals. Connect Penn's coordinate school structure to something you actually want to do across disciplines.

The "Why Penn" essay is also where you demonstrate that you understand Penn's culture. Penn has a distinctive pre-professional energy that is different from Yale or Brown — students are ambitious, career-oriented, and often already thinking about internships and recruiting during freshman year. If that culture genuinely appeals to you, say so and explain why. If it doesn't, that is worth reflecting on before you submit.

Penn's Pre-Professional Culture: Embrace It or Be Honest About It

Penn is one of the most pre-professional Ivy League universities. Wharton freshmen are often already thinking about investment banking or consulting recruiting. CAS students frequently pursue coordinated dual degree programs that add a Wharton or engineering component to their liberal arts degree. The career center is heavily used. Alumni networks in finance, consulting, and law are some of the strongest in the country.

This culture is an asset if it matches who you are — and it can be a liability if it doesn't. Students who thrive at Penn tend to be goal-oriented, socially ambitious, and genuinely interested in how ideas translate into professional outcomes. Students who want a more purely intellectual environment, or who are put off by the recruiting culture, often find Penn a poor fit — and admissions officers can often tell when an applicant's written profile suggests they would be happier somewhere else.

Coordinated Dual Degrees: A Distinctive Penn Feature

Beyond M&T, Penn offers a range of coordinated dual degree programs that let students combine Wharton with another school — life sciences, international studies, nursing, and more. These programs are competitive and selective, but they are also a genuine differentiator if they fit your goals.

If you are applying to one of these programs, your application needs to demonstrate why the combination is meaningful to you specifically. The admissions committee is not impressed by students who want both degrees because having two is better than one. They are looking for students who have a clear vision of what they want to do and have identified that this specific combination is the best path to get there.

What the Rest of Your Application Needs to Do

Penn's academic expectations are in line with other Ivies — strong GPA, rigorous coursework, competitive test scores. But Penn also has a clear preference for students who have already started to build something. The pre-professional culture means that evidence of real-world engagement — internships, business ventures, research with practical applications, leadership in organizations with genuine scope — reads particularly well in a Penn application.

Your recommenders should be able to speak to your intellectual engagement and your initiative. A recommendation that describes a student who sat in class and got good grades is weaker at Penn than at some other schools. A recommendation that describes a student who brought outside knowledge into the classroom, connected academic material to real-world questions, or took an idea from a course and pursued it independently — that resonates with what Penn values. Frequently Asked Questions

Is it easier to get into Penn Arts & Sciences than Wharton?

Not significantly. Penn's acceptance rate is roughly 7% overall, and while there is some variation by school, the difference is not large enough to justify applying to a school that doesn't fit your interests. More importantly, Penn readers are experienced at identifying applicants who chose a school for strategic reasons rather than genuine fit. If your entire profile signals business or finance but you apply to CAS to avoid Wharton competition, it reads as inauthentic — and that is a red flag, not an advantage.

How hard is it to get into the M&T program at Penn?

The Jerome Fisher Program in Management & Technology (M&T) is among the most selective programs at any university — acceptance rates are estimated well under 5% of an already-competitive Penn applicant pool. M&T admits students directly into a dual degree spanning Wharton and the School of Engineering and Applied Science. To be competitive, you need exceptional academic credentials across both math/science and business/leadership, a compelling reason why you need both disciplines simultaneously, and usually some evidence of entrepreneurship, research, or technical leadership.

How important is the "Why Penn" essay?

The "Why Penn" essay is one of the most important supplements you will write. Penn's admissions committee uses it to assess genuine interest and school knowledge — and they have high standards for what "specific" means. Mentioning Wharton's case-based learning or Penn's Quaker values is not specific enough. You need to reference faculty whose research connects to yours, particular programs like the Wharton Social Impact Initiative or Penn's dual degree options, or academic resources like the Pennovation Center. The essay should feel like it could only have been written about Penn.
