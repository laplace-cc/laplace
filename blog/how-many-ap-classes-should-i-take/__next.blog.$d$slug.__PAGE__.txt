<p>"How many APs do I need?" is one of the questions we hear most. The honest answer: there's no fixed number. Selective colleges care about rigor <strong>relative to what your school offers</strong>, and about how well you did in it.</p>

<h2>How colleges actually read your schedule</h2>
<p>Your school counselor submits a school profile that tells colleges which advanced courses are available. Admissions officers then ask: did this student take a challenging schedule, given their options? A student who takes six APs at a school that offers eight is read very differently from a student who takes six at a school that offers thirty.</p>
<p>Counselors are also often asked to rate the rigor of your schedule compared to your classmates. "Most demanding" is the rating that matters at highly selective schools.</p>

<h2>A reasonable range</h2>
<p>At high schools with a large AP program, students admitted to the most selective colleges commonly take several APs across junior and senior year, with many in their core subjects. But the specific count matters far less than these three things:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Core subjects first.</strong> Advanced English, math, science, history, and a world language carry more weight than extra electives.</li>
<li><strong>Grades.</strong> A schedule you can't keep strong grades in hurts more than it helps.</li>
<li><strong>Alignment with your interests.</strong> A future engineer should push hardest in math and physics; a future historian in humanities.</li>
</ul>

<h2>When to stop adding APs</h2>
<p>More isn't always better. Adding a fifth or sixth AP in a single year can crowd out the activities, research, and sleep that also make an application strong. If another AP would mean dropping a meaningful activity or risking grades in core courses, it's usually not worth it.</p>

<h2>AP vs. IB vs. dual enrollment</h2>
<p>All three show rigor. AP and IB are easiest for colleges to compare across applicants. Dual enrollment is most valuable when it takes you beyond what your school offers. Georgia students should read our <a href="/blog/georgia-dual-enrollment-guide/">Georgia Dual Enrollment guide</a>, and remember that rigor courses also count toward <a href="/blog/hope-vs-zell-miller-scholarship/">HOPE and Zell Miller</a>.</p>

<h2>Plan it as a four-year sequence</h2>
<p>The best schedules build steadily: honors courses early, then APs in your strongest areas, then the most demanding courses senior year. Dropping rigor senior year is a common mistake that colleges notice. Our <a href="/blog/junior-year-college-planning-timeline/">junior year timeline</a> shows when to lock in senior courses.</p>
