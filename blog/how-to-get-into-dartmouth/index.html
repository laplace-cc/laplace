How to Get Into Dartmouth: What the Admissions Office Is Really Looking For

Dartmouth admitted 6.2% of applicants in its most recent cycle — the highest acceptance rate in the Ivy League, though that framing is misleading. Dartmouth is the smallest Ivy by undergraduate enrollment, and the culture it is selecting for is more cohesive and specific than at larger peer schools. Getting into Dartmouth is not about checking boxes. It is about fitting a particular kind of intellectual and communal identity that Dartmouth has cultivated for over 250 years.

Understanding what makes Dartmouth genuinely different — not just smaller, but structurally and culturally distinct — is the foundation of any application strategy that actually works here.

The D-Plan: Dartmouth's Most Distinctive Feature

Dartmouth operates on a quarter system called the D-Plan, where students take courses in fall, winter, spring, and summer terms and can design their own academic calendar in ways that are impossible at semester-based universities. Students can spend a term abroad, do an off-campus internship, pursue independent research, or front-load their coursework to allow a longer sustained experience somewhere else. The D-Plan is not a gimmick — it genuinely reshapes how students think about their four years.

The admissions office knows this, and they look for students who would actually take advantage of it. If your "Why Dartmouth" essay does not engage with the D-Plan, you are missing one of the clearest signals you can send: that you have done real research, you understand what makes Dartmouth unusual, and you have thought about how its structure serves your goals. Be specific. What would your off-term look like? What would you do with the flexibility that a semester school cannot offer you?

The "Why Dartmouth" Essay: Specificity Is Everything

Of all the supplemental essays in the Ivy League, the "Why Dartmouth" essay may be the one where generic answers do the most damage. Dartmouth readers see thousands of applications that praise "the tight-knit community" and "small class sizes." Those phrases signal, correctly, that an applicant has not done serious research.

What actually works is reference to things that are unique to Dartmouth and real to you. The Dartmouth Outing Club — the oldest college outing club in the country — is not a talking point to drop in for points; it is worth mentioning only if you have actually looked into its trip offerings and can describe why that community appeals to you specifically. The same is true for DREAM (Dartmouth Reads, Engages, and Mentors), the Undergraduate Advising and Research program, or a specific faculty member whose published work you have read. The essay should read like it could only have been written by you, about Dartmouth. If it could have been written by anyone about any Ivy, start over.

Outdoor Culture and Physical Engagement

Dartmouth's location in Hanover, New Hampshire is not incidental to its character. The school attracts students who are genuinely drawn to outdoor experiences — skiing, hiking, kayaking, camping — and the campus culture reflects this in ways that are different from any other Ivy. Greek life is large and active (roughly 60% of eligible students are affiliated), and the social fabric of the school is tightly connected to both Greek houses and outdoor recreation.

This does not mean you need to be an avid mountaineer to get in. But it does mean that applicants who are genuinely excited about Dartmouth's physical culture — and can articulate that excitement authentically — tend to read as better fits than applicants who treat Dartmouth as an intellectually equivalent alternative to Yale. If outdoor life genuinely appeals to you, say so and be specific. If it does not, that is fine, but you need an equally compelling account of why Dartmouth specifically, not just why an Ivy.

Legacy: The Honest Picture

Dartmouth is one of the schools in the Ivy League where legacy status carries more weight than at most peer institutions. The alumni network is unusually loyal — Dartmouth consistently ranks among the top universities for alumni giving — and the admissions office is aware that legacy applicants come embedded in a community that sustains the institution. This is not unique to Dartmouth, but the magnitude is greater here because the school is small and the alumni base is deeply connected to campus life in an ongoing way.

If you are a legacy applicant, your application is not automatically strong — you still need competitive grades, test scores, and compelling essays. But the margin legacy provides is real, and you should not underestimate it. If you are not a legacy applicant, that is not a disadvantage per se, but you should understand that you are competing with a pool where a meaningful fraction of admitted students have that connection. The best response is a hyper-specific, deeply researched application that makes it impossible to dismiss your genuine interest.

The Alumni Interview: More Consequential Than at Most Schools

Dartmouth's alumni interviews matter more than interviews at most peer schools, for a simple reason: Dartmouth alumni are among the most devoted in higher education, and their assessments carry genuine weight with the admissions committee. An alumnus who has been interviewing students for 20 years has a sharp sense of who belongs at Dartmouth and who does not, and that sense gets communicated in their evaluation.

Prepare seriously. Know the D-Plan and be ready to talk about how you would use it. Have a clear answer to "why Dartmouth over the other schools you are applying to" that is specific and honest. Think about what you would contribute to the community — to a house, to a student organization, to a lab — not just what you would extract from the degree. The best interviews are conversations, not performances, and the best preparation is genuine engagement with what Dartmouth actually is.

Academic Fit: Dartmouth's Strengths and What They Signal

Dartmouth is genuinely exceptional in several areas: economics, government, engineering (through the Thayer School), neuroscience, and the arts and humanities. The Tuck School of Business offers an unusual modified master's program open to undergraduates. If your intended field of study aligns with one of Dartmouth's research strengths, make that connection explicit — name a specific professor, lab, or program and explain what you would do with access to it.

Dartmouth also has a strong undergraduate research culture through the Undergraduate Advising and Research (UGAR) office and programs like the Women in Science Project (WISP). Students who have already demonstrated research engagement in high school — through independent projects, summer programs, or competitions — are well-positioned to show how they would continue that work at Dartmouth. What Pulls an Application Together

The Dartmouth application that succeeds is one where everything points in the same direction: a student who has genuinely engaged with what Dartmouth is, who has a clear sense of what they want from their four years, and who can articulate why Dartmouth specifically — not an Ivy generically — serves those goals. The D-Plan, the outdoor culture, the Greek life, the alumni community: these are not boxes to check. They are real features of a real place, and the application that treats them as such stands out from the very first page.

Start by spending real time with Dartmouth's website, student blogs, and published research from faculty you might work with. Then write the "Why Dartmouth" essay last, after everything else is done, so it reflects the full coherence of your application rather than an isolated attempt to sound interested. Frequently Asked Questions

Does legacy status really help at Dartmouth more than at other Ivies?

Yes — Dartmouth is widely considered to be among the Ivy League schools where legacy status carries the most weight in admissions. Because Dartmouth's alumni network is unusually tight-knit and the school is the smallest Ivy, legacy applicants are evaluated with particular attention. This does not mean legacy alone is enough — you still need a competitive academic profile and compelling essays — but the margin it provides is real and larger than at schools like Harvard or Yale, where the applicant pools are so deep that legacy is less decisive.

How important is the Dartmouth alumni interview?

Dartmouth's alumni interviews are among the most consequential in the Ivy League. Because Dartmouth alumni are famously devoted to their school, the interviewer often has strong opinions about what makes a good Dartmouth student — and those opinions feed into the evaluation. A strong interview can move the needle in a way that is unusual compared to peer schools. Prepare thoroughly: know what the D-Plan is and why it appeals to you, have specific thoughts on Dartmouth's undergraduate culture, and be ready to articulate what you would contribute to the community.

What should the "Why Dartmouth" essay focus on?

The "Why Dartmouth" essay rewards extreme specificity. Generic statements about "small class sizes" and "close-knit community" are the single most common failure mode. Instead, reference things that are actually unique to Dartmouth: a specific D-Plan term you would use for an off-campus experience, a professor whose research you have read, a particular program like the Dartmouth Outing Club or DREAM, or a house community you are drawn to. The more specific your answer, the more it signals genuine interest rather than Dartmouth as a safety net on an Ivy wishlist.
